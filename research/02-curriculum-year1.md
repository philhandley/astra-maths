## 1. Statutory programme of study (verbatim)

The gov.uk page says: "Schools are not required by law to teach the example content in [square brackets] or the content indicated as being 'non-statutory'." The bracketed examples are printed in full below but are not statutory.

### Number: number and place value
- **Y1-NPV-1** "count to and across 100, forwards and backwards, beginning with 0 or 1, or from any given number"
- **Y1-NPV-2** "count, read and write numbers to 100 in numerals; count in multiples of 2s, 5s and 10s"
- **Y1-NPV-3** "given a number, identify 1 more and 1 less"
- **Y1-NPV-4** "identify and represent numbers using objects and pictorial representations including the number line, and use the language of: equal to, more than, less than (fewer), most, least"
- **Y1-NPV-5** "read and write numbers from 1 to 20 in numerals and words"

### Number: addition and subtraction
- **Y1-AS-1** "read, write and interpret mathematical statements involving addition (+), subtraction (−) and equals (=) signs"
- **Y1-AS-2** "represent and use number bonds and related subtraction facts within 20"
- **Y1-AS-3** "add and subtract one-digit and two-digit numbers to 20, including 0"
- **Y1-AS-4** "solve one-step problems that involve addition and subtraction, using concrete objects and pictorial representations, and missing number problems such as 7 = ? − 9"

### Number: multiplication and division
- **Y1-MD-1** "solve one-step problems involving multiplication and division, by calculating the answer using concrete objects, pictorial representations and arrays with the support of the teacher"

### Number: fractions
- **Y1-F-1** "recognise, find and name a half as 1 of 2 equal parts of an object, shape or quantity"
- **Y1-F-2** "recognise, find and name a quarter as 1 of 4 equal parts of an object, shape or quantity"

### Measurement
- **Y1-M-1** "compare, describe and solve practical problems for: lengths and heights [for example, long/short, longer/shorter, tall/short, double/half]; mass/weight [for example, heavy/light, heavier than, lighter than]; capacity and volume [for example, full/empty, more than, less than, half, half full, quarter]; time [for example, quicker, slower, earlier, later]"
- **Y1-M-2** "measure and begin to record the following: lengths and heights; mass/weight; capacity and volume; time (hours, minutes, seconds)"
- **Y1-M-3** "recognise and know the value of different denominations of coins and notes"
- **Y1-M-4** "sequence events in chronological order using language [for example, before and after, next, first, today, yesterday, tomorrow, morning, afternoon and evening]"
- **Y1-M-5** "recognise and use language relating to dates, including days of the week, weeks, months and years"
- **Y1-M-6** "tell the time to the hour and half past the hour and draw the hands on a clock face to show these times"

### Geometry: properties of shapes
- **Y1-G-1** "recognise and name common 2-D and 3-D shapes, including: 2-D shapes [for example, rectangles (including squares), circles and triangles]; 3-D shapes [for example, cuboids (including cubes), pyramids and spheres]"

### Geometry: position and direction
- **Y1-PD-1** "describe position, direction and movement, including whole, half, quarter and three-quarter turns"

## 2. Non-statutory notes and guidance (paraphrased)

These paraphrase the "Notes and guidance (non-statutory)" text printed under each Year 1 strand on gov.uk.

**Number and place value.** Pupils practise counting in ones, ordering (first, second, third) and using numbers to show a quantity, such as 3 apples or 2 centimetres, including simple concrete problems, until they are fluent. They begin to see place value beyond 20 by reading, writing, counting and comparing numbers up to 100 with objects and pictures. They practise counting as reciting numbers and as counting objects, and count in 2s, 5s and 10s from different starting points to spot patterns such as odd and even numbers. They recognise and make repeating patterns with objects and shapes.

**Addition and subtraction.** Pupils memorise and reason with number bonds to 10 and 20 in several forms, for example 9 + 7 = 16, 16 − 7 = 9 and 7 = 16 − 9. They learn that adding or subtracting 0 changes nothing, which shows that addition and subtraction are related operations. They combine and increase numbers by counting forwards and backwards. Problems in familiar practical contexts use words such as put together, altogether, take away, distance between, difference between, more than and less than.

**Multiplication and division.** Through grouping and sharing small quantities, pupils begin to understand multiplication and division, doubling, and finding simple fractions of objects, numbers and quantities. They connect arrays, number patterns and counting in 2s, 5s and 10s.

**Fractions.** Pupils learn half and quarter as "fractions of" both discrete and continuous quantities, by solving problems with shapes, objects and quantities, such as finding half of a length, a set of objects or a shape. They connect halves and quarters to equal sharing and grouping, to measures, and to combining halves and quarters into a whole.

**Measurement.** At this stage mass and weight, and volume and capacity, are used interchangeably. Pupils move from non-standard units, including counting and liquids, to manageable common standard units, using rulers, weighing scales and containers. They use the language of time, first o'clock and then half past.

**Geometry: properties of shapes.** Pupils handle common 2-D and 3-D shapes and name them and related everyday objects fluently. They recognise shapes in different orientations and sizes, and know that rectangles, triangles, cuboids and pyramids are not always similar to each other.

**Geometry: position and direction.** Pupils use the language of position, direction and motion, such as left and right, top, middle and bottom, on top of, in front of, above, between, around, near and far, up and down, forwards and backwards, and inside and outside. They make whole, half, quarter and three-quarter turns in both directions and connect clockwise turns with movement on a clock face.

## 3. DfE ready-to-progress criteria for Year 1 (verbatim, with code)

The criterion text is taken from the detailed criterion pages of the DfE guidance. Figures are images in the PDF, so only their captions are given. The symbols in the 1AS–2 criterion were lost when the PDF text was extracted; they are restored here as (+), (−) and (=), which the statutory programme uses in the same place. The summary table also lists previous experience and future applications for each criterion; the extracted text does not keep those columns apart, so they are not transcribed here.

**1NPV–1** "Count within 100, forwards and backwards, starting with any number."
- Representations: a 100 square (Figure 1), a 0 to 100 number line (Figure 2), a Gattegno chart of multiples of 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 (Figure 3), and a partly marked 0 to 100 number line for backwards counting (Figure 4).
- Practice should include reciting number names without visual support, counting while pointing at numerals on a 100 square or number line (or tapping a Gattegno chart), and starting from numbers other than 1 or 100.
- Dual counting: count with number names, then repeat the count using words that show number structure. Language focus: "…seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen… twenty, twenty-one, twenty-two…" and "…seven, eight, nine, one-ten, one-ten-one, one-ten-two, one-ten-three… two-tens, two-tens-one, two-tens two…"
- Backwards counting is harder after a multiple of 10. The guidance says counting back from 20 to 10 "requires additional focused practice, due to the irregularity of these number names". It also says: "By the end of year 1, pupils must be able to count forwards and backwards, within 100, without visual aids."

**1NPV–2** "Reason about the location of numbers to 20 within the linear number system, including comparing using < > and ="
- Introduce the number line as a representation of order and relative size. Pupils draw number lines with equally spaced consecutive whole numbers, and place numbers up to 20 on marked and unmarked lines. Figure 5 marks 5, 12 and 19 on a marked 0 to 20 line.
- Language focus: "a is 5 because it is halfway between 0 and 10." "b is 12 because it is 2 more than 10." "c is 19 because it is one less than 20." Efficient strategies include working backwards from a multiple of 10.
- On unmarked lines, pupils estimate using proportional reasoning, starting with 0 to 10, then 0 to 20 and 10 to 20, and mark the midpoint first. Example: "16 is about here because it is just over halfway between 10 and 20." Accuracy is not required; "reasonable judgements" are (Figures 6 and 7).
- Example tasks: label 9, 15, 3 and 12 on a number line; estimate missing numbers on unmarked lines; use 10 cm paper strips to estimate lengths in the classroom; measure two leaves with a ruler.

**1NF–1** "Develop fluency in addition and subtraction facts within 10."
- "It is very important for pupils to be able to add and subtract within 10, fluently, by the end of year 1." Teaching continues until pupils move past counting in ones to efficient strategies and then to automatic recall.
- "The 66 addition facts within 10 are shown on the grid below." Commutativity reduces the number to learn, because 3 + 2 is the same as 2 + 3. Pupils must also recall the matching subtraction facts automatically, for example 5 – 3 and 5 – 2.
- Derived facts, shown with tens frames and counters (Figures 8 and 9). Language focus: "I know that double 3 is equal to 6, so 4 plus 3 is equal to 7." "If I subtract 2 from an even number I get the previous even number, so 6 minus 2 is equal to 4."
- Contextual problems within 10 (the example questions are word problems only): cycling 4 km and then 3 km more; 9 children, 6 with scooters; Sarah has £6 and spends £3; 1 metre of red ribbon and 5 metres of blue ribbon. "Pupils need extensive practice to meet this criterion."

**1NF–2** "Count forwards and backwards in multiples of 2, 5 and 10, up to 10 multiples, beginning with any multiple, and count forwards and backwards through the odd numbers."
- Practice should include reciting just the number names ("ten, twenty, thirty…") without visual support; counting on number lines, a 100 square or a Gattegno chart; and starting forward counts from numbers other than 2, 5 or 10. Number lines for 2s, 10s and 5s are Figures 10, 11 and 12. The odd-number sequence is Figure 14.
- Skip counting equal groups: pupils identify how many objects are in each group, then skip count in that number. Language focus: "The pencils are in groups of 10, so we will count in tens." (Figure 13 shows 9 packs of 10 pencils.) Later this is shortened to "1 ten, 2 tens, 3 tens…".
- Pupils practise counting in two ways: skip counting to find the total, or counting the number of equal groups. The guidance says: "Recognising that a group of 5, for example, can be treated as a single unit is called unitising, and is the basis of multiplicative reasoning."
- Coins: once pupils know 2p, 5p and 10p coins, they count sets of like coins and find how many coins of one kind pay for an item. The example task asks for a drum paid for with 2p coins, a boat with 5p coins and a dinosaur with 10p coins.

**1AS–1** "Compose numbers to 10 from 2 parts, and partition numbers to 10 into parts, including recognising odd and even numbers."
- Varied representations of 8 as two parts: 3 fingers and 5 fingers (Figure 15); 6 and 2 on base 10 number boards (Figure 16); two 4-value dice (Figure 17); 2 rows of 4 (Figure 18); tally marks 5 and 3 (Figure 19); a bead string with 7 and 1 (Figure 20).
- Partitioning diagrams are used with quantity images. Language focus: "There are 6 flags. 4 are spotty and 2 are stripy." "6 is the whole. 4 is a part. 2 is a part." (Figure 21.) The guidance says partitioning "underpins subtraction", and that exploring partitions "helps pupils to understand that addition and subtraction are inverse operations".
- Work systematically: as one part goes up by 1, the other goes down by 1, and pairs repeat with the values reversed (2 and 4, then 4 and 2). This lays the foundation for commutativity (Figure 22). Pupils must be able to describe these patterns.
- Odd and even numbers up to 10 are recognised by whether they can be made from groups of 2. Base 10 number boards or tens frames with counters arranged in twos show this structure (Figure 23).

**1AS–2** "Read, write and interpret equations containing addition (+), subtraction (−) and equals (=) symbols, and relate additive expressions and equations to real-life contexts."
- The four additive structures are aggregation (combining 2 parts to make 1 whole), partitioning (separating 1 whole into 2 parts), augmentation (increasing a quantity by adding more) and reduction (decreasing a quantity by taking some away). Language focus, aggregation: "There are 5 flowers in one bunch. There are 2 flowers in the other bunch. There are 7 flowers altogether." "We can write this as 5 plus 2 is equal to 7."
- Language focus, partitioning: "There are 6 children altogether. 2 children are wearing coats. 4 children are not wearing coats." "We can write this as 6 minus 2 is equal to 4." Language focus, reduction: "First there were 4 children in the bumper car. Then 1 child got out. Now there are 3 children in the bumper car." "We can write this as 4 minus 1 is equal to 3."
- Teaching points: "It is important that pupils do not think of the equals symbol as meaning 'and the answer is'." "'take away' should only be used to describe the subtraction operation in reduction contexts." Pupils describe the context first, then write the expression or equation, then say what each number represents.
- Diagrams to link with equations: bar model (Figure 29), number line (Figure 30), tens frame with counters (Figure 31) and cherry partitioning model (Figure 32). Equations can vary in form, for example the equals sign in different positions, and addends in either order (commutativity). All examples use quantities within 10. Correct calculation is not required for this criterion; that is assessed under 1NF–1.

**1G–1** "Recognise common 2D and 3D shapes presented in different orientations, and know that rectangles, triangles, cuboids and pyramids are not always similar to one another."
- Pupils must recognise and name at least rectangles (including squares), circles and triangles, and cuboids (including cubes), cylinders, spheres and pyramids. Explore physical shapes, such as shapes cut from card, rather than pictures alone.
- Recognise shapes in varied orientations, sizes and proportions, including large shapes outside, such as a rectangle marked on a playground. Pupils describe non-similar examples informally, for example "long and thin", and recognise they are still the same shape (Figure 33, non-similar cylinders).
- Distinguish a named shape from plausible distractors (Figure 34). Language focus: "Shape a: 'This is not a triangle because it has 4 sides.'" "Shape b or e: 'This is a triangle because it has 3 straight sides.'" "Shape c or d: 'This is not a triangle because it has 6 sides.'" "Shape f: 'This is not a triangle because some sides are curved.'"

**1G–2** "Compose 2D and 3D shapes from smaller shapes to match an example, including manipulating shapes to place them in particular orientations."
- 2D: pattern blocks and tangrams. At first the pieces match the example's size and colour, and pupils lay them over it. By the end of year 1 they copy a pattern block picture and make "a good attempt at copying a tangram picture, without overlaying the pieces on the example" (Figures 35 and 36). Tangrams are harder: several triangle sizes must be told apart, and the parallelogram may need turning over, not just rotating.
- 3D: join a given number of multilink blocks to match an example. Pupils first make their own shape, then compare shapes. The guidance lists the spatial language to use: left, right, top, middle, bottom, on top of, below, in front of, behind and between (Figure 37). Later pupils copy compound shapes made from cuboids, cylinders, spheres and pyramids (Figure 38).
- Assessment tasks: copy a shape made from multilink cubes; copy a pattern block picture; copy a tower built from building blocks; copy a pattern made from identical equilateral triangles. The guidance says practical work in small groups "provides the most reliable method of assessing" this criterion.

### What Year 1 pupils should be fluent in by the end of the year
- **Addition and subtraction within 10 (1NF–1).** Fluency is required "by the end of year 1", with automatic recall of facts such as 5 – 3 and 5 – 2. The guidance names no separate shortlist of facts. It refers to the full grid of 66 addition facts within 10, and to derived-fact strategies such as double 3, near-doubles and subtracting 2 from an even number.
- **Inverse facts.** The calculation section says: "if a pupil knows 6 + 4 = 10, then they should be able to reason that 10 – 4 = 6 and 10 – 6 = 4." Pupils should also solve contextual addition and subtraction within 10 using the four structures (aggregation, partitioning, augmentation and reduction).
- **Counting in 2s, 5s and 10s (1NF–2).** "Pupils must be fluent in counting in multiples of 2, 5 and 10 by the end of year 1."
- **Counting within 100 without visual aids (1NPV–1), and odd and even numbers up to 10 (1AS–1).**
- **Vocabulary note.** The statutory Year 1 multiplication and division statement uses those words, but the 1NF–2 guidance says pupils "do not need to be introduced to the words 'multiplication' and 'division' or to the multiplication and division symbols (× and ÷) in year 1".

## 5. End-of-Year-1 checklist for a parent

- Can count forwards and backwards within 100, starting from any number, without pictures or a number line. [Y1-NPV-1; 1NPV–1]
- Can count in 2s, 5s and 10s from any starting number, and count forwards and backwards through odd numbers. [Y1-NPV-2; 1NF–2]
- Can read and write numbers to 100 in digits, and numbers from 1 to 20 in words. [Y1-NPV-2; Y1-NPV-5]
- Can say which number is 1 more and which is 1 less than a given number, and can place numbers to 20 on a number line to compare them with < > and =. [Y1-NPV-3; Y1-NPV-4; 1NPV–2]
- Can add and subtract within 10 quickly from memory, for example 5 + 3, 8 − 5 and the pairs that make 10. [1NF–1; Y1-AS-2]
- Can add and subtract one-digit and two-digit numbers to 20, including 0, and can find a missing number such as 7 = ? − 9. [Y1-AS-3; Y1-AS-4]
- Can read and write number sentences such as 6 − 2 = 4, and explain what each number means in a story. [Y1-AS-1; 1AS–2]
- Can split a number to 10 into two parts, put the parts back together, and say whether it is odd or even. [1AS–1]
- Can solve simple problems about equal groups by skip counting or with pictures and arrays. [Y1-MD-1; 1NF–2]
- Can find a half and a quarter of an object, a shape or a set of things. [Y1-F-1; Y1-F-2]
- Can name coins and notes and their values, and tell the time to the hour and half past, drawing the hands on a clock face. [Y1-M-3; Y1-M-6]
- Can compare and describe lengths, heights, weight, capacity and time, and measure with a ruler or scales. [Y1-M-1; Y1-M-2]
- Can put events in order, and use words for days, weeks, months and years, and words such as yesterday and tomorrow. [Y1-M-4; Y1-M-5]
- Can name common 2-D and 3-D shapes in different sizes and positions, and explain why a shape is or is not a triangle. [Y1-G-1; 1G–1]
- Can build a shape from smaller shapes to match a picture, and describe whole, half, quarter and three-quarter turns. [1G–2; Y1-PD-1]
