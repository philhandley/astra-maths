## 1. Statutory programme of study (verbatim)

### Number: number and place value
- **Y2-NPV-1** "count in steps of 2, 3, and 5 from 0, and in 10s from any number, forward and backward"
- **Y2-NPV-2** "recognise the place value of each digit in a two-digit number (10s, 1s)"
- **Y2-NPV-3** "identify, represent and estimate numbers using different representations, including the number line"
- **Y2-NPV-4** "compare and order numbers from 0 up to 100; use <, > and = signs"
- **Y2-NPV-5** "read and write numbers to at least 100 in numerals and in words"
- **Y2-NPV-6** "use place value and number facts to solve problems"

### Number: addition and subtraction
- **Y2-AS-1** "solve problems with addition and subtraction:"
  - "using concrete objects and pictorial representations, including those involving numbers, quantities and measures"
  - "applying their increasing knowledge of mental and written methods"
- **Y2-AS-2** "recall and use addition and subtraction facts to 20 fluently, and derive and use related facts up to 100"
- **Y2-AS-3** "add and subtract numbers using concrete objects, pictorial representations, and mentally, including:"
  - "a two-digit number and 1s"
  - "a two-digit number and 10s"
  - "2 two-digit numbers"
  - "adding 3 one-digit numbers"
- **Y2-AS-4** "show that addition of 2 numbers can be done in any order (commutative) and subtraction of 1 number from another cannot"
- **Y2-AS-5** "recognise and use the inverse relationship between addition and subtraction and use this to check calculations and solve missing number problems"

### Number: multiplication and division
- **Y2-MD-1** "recall and use multiplication and division facts for the 2, 5 and 10 multiplication tables, including recognising odd and even numbers"
- **Y2-MD-2** "calculate mathematical statements for multiplication and division within the multiplication tables and write them using the multiplication (×), division (÷) and equals (=) signs"
- **Y2-MD-3** "show that multiplication of 2 numbers can be done in any order (commutative) and division of 1 number by another cannot"
- **Y2-MD-4** "solve problems involving multiplication and division, using materials, arrays, repeated addition, mental methods, and multiplication and division facts, including problems in contexts"

### Number: fractions
- **Y2-F-1** "recognise, find, name and write fractions 1/3, 1/4, 2/4 and 3/4 of a length, shape, set of objects or quantity"
- **Y2-F-2** "write simple fractions, for example 1/2 of 6 = 3 and recognise the equivalence of 2/4 and 1/2"

### Measurement
- **Y2-M-1** "choose and use appropriate standard units to estimate and measure length/height in any direction (m/cm); mass (kg/g); temperature (°C); capacity (litres/ml) to the nearest appropriate unit, using rulers, scales, thermometers and measuring vessels"
- **Y2-M-2** "compare and order lengths, mass, volume/capacity and record the results using >, < and ="
- **Y2-M-3** "recognise and use symbols for pounds (£) and pence (p); combine amounts to make a particular value"
- **Y2-M-4** "find different combinations of coins that equal the same amounts of money"
- **Y2-M-5** "solve simple problems in a practical context involving addition and subtraction of money of the same unit, including giving change"
- **Y2-M-6** "compare and sequence intervals of time"
- **Y2-M-7** "tell and write the time to five minutes, including quarter past/to the hour and draw the hands on a clock face to show these times"
- **Y2-M-8** "know the number of minutes in an hour and the number of hours in a day"

### Geometry: properties of shapes
- **Y2-G-1** "identify and describe the properties of 2-D shapes, including the number of sides, and line symmetry in a vertical line"
- **Y2-G-2** "identify and describe the properties of 3-D shapes, including the number of edges, vertices and faces"
- **Y2-G-3** "identify 2-D shapes on the surface of 3-D shapes, [for example, a circle on a cylinder and a triangle on a pyramid]"
- **Y2-G-4** "compare and sort common 2-D and 3-D shapes and everyday objects"

### Geometry: position and direction
- **Y2-PD-1** "order and arrange combinations of mathematical objects in patterns and sequences"
- **Y2-PD-2** "use mathematical vocabulary to describe position, direction and movement, including movement in a straight line and distinguishing between rotation as a turn and in terms of right angles for quarter, half and three-quarter turns (clockwise and anti-clockwise)"

### Statistics
- **Y2-S-1** "interpret and construct simple pictograms, tally charts, block diagrams and tables"
- **Y2-S-2** "ask and answer simple questions by counting the number of objects in each category and sorting the categories by quantity"
- **Y2-S-3** "ask-and-answer questions about totalling and comparing categorical data"

## 2. Non-statutory notes and guidance (paraphrased)

**Number: number and place value.** Pupils use materials and representations to count, read, write and compare numbers to at least 100 until they are fluent, and they count in 3s to prepare for later work on thirds. As they grow confident, they meet larger numbers and look for patterns in the number system, including spatial representations. They partition numbers in different ways (23 = 20 + 3 and 23 = 10 + 13) to support subtraction, and begin to see 0 as a place holder.

**Number: addition and subtraction.** Pupils learn the words "sum" and "difference". They practise facts to 20 until they can derive related facts, such as 3 + 7 = 10, 10 − 7 = 3 and 7 = 10 − 3, and apply them to 30 + 70 = 100. They check calculations by adding to check a subtraction and by reordering additions, which establishes commutativity and associativity. Recording in columns supports place value and leads to formal written methods with larger numbers later.

**Number: multiplication and division.** Pupils use a range of language and practise the 2, 5 and 10 times tables until they are fluent, connecting them to each other, to place value (the 10 table) and to the divisions on a clock face (the 5 table). They then begin other tables and related division facts. Work with materials covers grouping, sharing, arrays and repeated addition, and links to fractions and measures (40 ÷ 2 = 20, so 20 is a half of 40). Commutativity and inverse relations build multiplicative reasoning (4 × 5 = 20 and 20 ÷ 5 = 4).

**Number: fractions.** Fractions are taught as "fractions of" discrete and continuous quantities, using shapes, objects and quantities. Unit fractions link to equal sharing and grouping, and to measures. 3/4 is the first non-unit fraction met. Pupils count in fractions up to 10 from any starting point, using the 1/2 = 2/4 equivalence on a number line, which shows that fractions can add up to more than 1.

**Measurement.** Pupils use standard units with increasing accuracy, the correct language and standard abbreviations. They compare measures, including simple multiples such as "half as high" and "twice as wide". They become fluent at telling and recording time on analogue clocks, and at counting and recognising coins. They read and say amounts of money with £ and p, recording pounds and pence separately.

**Geometry: properties of shapes.** Pupils handle and name common 2-D and 3-D shapes, including quadrilaterals, other polygons, cuboids, prisms and cones, and identify their properties such as number of sides and faces. They use precise vocabulary (sides, edges, vertices, faces) and sort and compare shapes by their properties. They draw lines and shapes with a straight edge.

**Geometry: position and direction.** Pupils work with patterns of shapes in different orientations. They use the concept and language of angles to describe a turn, including practical work where they move in turns, give turning instructions to each other, or program robots with right-angle instructions.

**Statistics.** Pupils record, interpret, collate, organise and compare information, including pictograms where one symbol stands for several items (many-to-one correspondence, with simple ratios of 2, 5 and 10).

## 3. DfE ready-to-progress criteria for Year 2 (verbatim, with code)

Criterion wording is from the DfE summary table (PDF pp. 3–7). Guidance is from the Year 2 chapter (pp. 12–38), summarised with quotes where the PDF gives them. The PDF's figures are identified by their captions only; they were not viewed as images.

### 2NPV–1: Place value in two-digit numbers (guidance pp. 12–14)
**Criterion:** "Recognise the place value of each digit in two-digit numbers, and compose and decompose two-digit numbers using standard and non-standard partitioning."
- Pupils identify the total in different representations of groups of ten and extra ones, with the positions of tens and ones varied (Figure 1). "Pupils should recognise that 42, for example, can be composed either of 42 ones, or of 4 tens and 2 ones."
- Language focus: "This is the number 42. The 4 shows we have 4 groups of ten. The 2 shows we have 2 extra ones."
- Partition into tens and ones with diagrams and equations, for example 28 into 20 and 8 (Figure 2).
- Flexible partitioning: "partition into a multiple of ten and another two-digit number, in different ways (for example, 68 can be partitioned into 50 and 18, into 40 and 28, and so on)", and "partition into a two-digit number and a one-digit number, in different ways" (68 into 67 and 1, or 66 and 2).

### 2NPV–2: Two-digit numbers in the linear number system (guidance pp. 14–16)
**Criterion:** "Reason about the location of any two-digit number in the linear number system, including identifying the previous and next multiple of 10."
- Place two-digit numbers on a marked 0 to 100 number line, working backwards from a multiple of 10 where useful (Figure 3 places 36 and 79).
- Language focus: "a is 36 because it is one more than the midpoint of 35." and "b is 79 because it is one less than 80."
- On an unmarked 0 to 100 line, estimate with proportional reasoning, not by counting on: "60 is about here on the number line because it's just over half way" (Figure 4).
- Identify which two multiples of 10 a number lies between, to prepare for rounding.

### 2NF–1: Fluency in addition and subtraction facts within 10 (guidance pp. 16–18)
**Criterion:** "Secure fluency in addition and subtraction facts within 10, through continued practice."
- The guidance shows the 66 addition facts within 10 on a grid. Commutativity reduces what has to be learnt (3 + 2 is the same as 2 + 3). Pupils also need automatic recall of the matching subtraction facts, such as 5 − 3 and 5 − 2.
- Pupils "should practise additive calculation within 10 until they have automatic recall of the additive facts". This fluency is needed for 2AS–1, 2AS–3 and 2AS–4.
- Assessment: pupils must add and subtract within 10 "without counting forwards or backwards in ones on their fingers, on a number line or in their heads". Assess in small groups, because "simply providing the correct answers to calculations in a written test does not demonstrate that a pupil has met the criterion".
- No language focus box is given for this criterion.

### 2AS–1: Add and subtract across 10 (guidance pp. 18–20)
**Criterion:** "Add and subtract across 10."
- Addition: pupils learn to add three one-digit numbers by making 10 first (for example, 7 + 3 + 2 = 10 + 2), then add across 10 by partitioning one addend (7 + 5 = 7 + 3 + 2). Figure 5 shows tens frames and a partitioning diagram for 7 + 5 = 12.
- Subtraction, two strategies (Figures 6 and 7, both for 15 − 9): "subtracting through 10" partitions the subtrahend (15 − 5 = 10, then 10 − 4 = 6); "subtracting from 10" partitions the minuend (10 − 9 = 1, then add the 5 that 15 has above 10, giving 6).
- Tens frames and partitioning diagrams support the strategies at first, but "they should not be using the manipulatives as a tool for finding answers".
- Doubles and halves help: pupils should recall doubles of 6, 7, 8 and 9 and halves of 12, 14, 16 and 18 (for example, 6 + 6 = 12 and 18 − 9 = 9). No language focus box is given.

### 2AS–2: Difference and "How many more…?" (guidance pp. 20–23)
**Criterion:** "Recognise the subtraction structure of ‘difference’ and answer questions of the form, “How many more…?”."
- Problems phrased as "find the difference", "how many more" and "how many fewer" can be solved by a missing-addend equation or by subtraction, using known facts and strategies.
- Worked example from the guidance: "There are 5 red cars and 3 blue cars. What is the difference between the number of red cars and blue cars?" Pupils should understand they need to calculate a missing addend (3 + □ = 5) or the subtraction 5 − 3.
- Figure 8 shows a bar model and a number line for finding a difference.
- Example question: "I have £19 and want to buy a game which costs £25. How much more money do I need?"

### 2AS–3: Within 100, ones or tens only (guidance pp. 23–27)
**Criterion:** "Add and subtract within 100 by applying related one-digit addition and subtraction facts: add and subtract only ones or only tens to/from a two-digit number."
- One-digit facts are applied to: tens and tens (40 + 30 = 70, and 70 − 30 = 40); ones and a two-digit number (64 + 3 = 67, and 67 − 3 = 64); tens and a two-digit number (45 + 30 = 75, and 75 − 30 = 45); and ones subtracted from a multiple of 10 using complements of 10 (30 − 3 = 27, because 10 − 3 = 7).
- Dienes, tens frames and partitioning diagrams link these calculations to one-digit facts. Figure 9 uses Dienes with equations; Figure 10 uses tens frames and number lines for subtracting ones from a multiple of 10.
- Language focus: "4 plus 3 is equal to 7. So 4 tens and plus 3 tens is equal to 7 tens." and "10 minus 3 is equal to 7. So 30 minus 3 is equal to 27."
- Assessment-section language focus: "I know that 2 plus 5 is equal to 7, so 20 plus 50 is equal to 70. There are 9 ones as well, so 20 plus 59 is equal to 79." Pupils who sketch "jumping back" in tens on a number line to solve 90 − 40 "have not yet met criterion 2AS–3".

### 2AS–4: Any two two-digit numbers (guidance pp. 27–29)
**Criterion:** "Add and subtract within 100 by applying related one-digit addition and subtraction facts: add and subtract any 2 two-digit numbers."
- Addition: partition both numbers, or partition only one. For 45 + 23: 40 + 20 = 60, 5 + 3 = 8, 60 + 8 = 68; or 45 + 20 + 3 = 65 + 3 = 68. Figures 17 and 18 show 26 + 37 both ways.
- Language focus: "First I partition both numbers. Then I add the tens. Then I add the ones. Then I combine all of the tens and all of the ones."
- Subtraction: partition one number only. For 45 − 23: 45 − 20 − 3 = 25 − 3 = 22, or 45 − 3 − 20 = 42 − 20 = 22. Figures 19 and 20 show 63 − 17 done with the tens first or the ones first. The guidance warns against partitioning both numbers for subtraction, because it "can lead to errors, or calculations involving negative numbers, when bridging a multiple of 10" (for example, 63 − 17 is 60 − 10 + 3 − 7, not 60 − 10 + 7 − 3).
- Formal written methods are not needed in Year 2, but column layout under tens and ones headings, with counters or dots, "could be used as an alternative way to record two-digit calculations".

### 2MD–1: Repeated addition and the 2, 5 and 10 tables (guidance pp. 30–33)
**Criterion:** "Recognise repeated addition contexts, representing them with multiplication equations and calculating the product, within the 2, 5 and 10 multiplication tables."
- Equal groups first: 3 groups of 5 eggs (Figure 12). Language focus: "There are 3 equal groups of eggs." "There are 5 eggs in each group." "There are 3 groups of 5."
- Repeated addition and multiplication: 5 + 5 + 5 = 3 × 5. "The 3 represents the number of groups." "The 5 represents the number of eggs in each group." "The 15 represents the total number of eggs." Pupils must be able to explain how each term links to the context.
- When the total cannot be seen, three 5-value counters can represent 3 × 5 (Figure 13).
- Skip count in 2s, 5s and 10s up to 10 groups "until they are fluent", keeping track with fingers or tallies. Commutativity lets pupils work out 2 sevens from 7 twos.

### 2MD–2: Missing factors and division (guidance pp. 33–35)
**Criterion:** "Relate grouping problems where the number of groups is unknown to multiplication equations with a missing factor, and to division equations (quotitive division)."
- Biscuit example: "There are 15 biscuits. If I put them into bags of 5, how many bags will I need?" is written as □ × 5 = 15 and solved by skip counting or from multiplication-table fluency (Figure 14: three bags of 5 biscuits beside three 5-value counters).
- Division: 15 ÷ 5 = 3, described as "division into groups". Language focus: "The 15 represents the total number of biscuits." "The 5 represents the number of biscuits in each bag." "The 3 represents the number of bags." "15 divided into groups of 5 is equal to 3."
- Division without a context, such as 60 ÷ 10, is found by counting in tens or from the fact 6 × 10 = 60.
- Example question: "Miss Robinson asked Harry to get 60 apples from the kitchen. The apples come in bags of 10. How many bags does Harry need to get?"

### 2G–1: Describing and comparing 2D and 3D shapes (guidance pp. 35–38)
**Criterion:** "Use precise language to describe the properties of 2D and 3D shapes, and compare shapes by reasoning about similarities and differences in properties."
- 2-D: pupils use "sides" and "vertex/vertices" and count each side (or vertex) "once and only once". A polygon is a 2-D shape with only straight sides, and its number of sides decides its type, not its look (Figure 15: an irregular hexagon). Language focus: "This shape is a hexagon because it has exactly 6 straight sides."
- Varied exemplars: pupils should "explore shapes (for example, shapes cut from card) rather than only looking at pictures", including irregular polygons with some equal sides, some equal angles, or pairs of parallel or perpendicular sides (Figure 16). Language focus: "These shapes are all pentagons because they all have exactly 5 straight sides."
- 3-D: pupils count "edges", "vertex/vertices" and "faces" on simple shapes such as a triangular-based pyramid or a cuboid, using sticky paper if needed, and identify the 2-D shapes that make up the faces.
- Informal language for the space inside 2-D shapes ("long and thin", "short and wide", "larger", "smaller") prepares for area. Practical work in small groups "should form part of the assessment of this criterion".

### Standard and non-standard partitioning (the idea, with examples)
- Standard partitioning splits a number by place value. The DfE's own example is 28 into 20 and 8 (Figure 2). For 68, the place-value split is 60 + 8 (this example is ours).
- Non-standard partitioning splits the same number in other ways. The DfE gives 68 as 50 and 18, or 40 and 28, or 67 and 1, or 66 and 2. The non-statutory notes give 23 = 10 + 13 as well as 23 = 20 + 3.
- The purpose is stated in the guidance: pupils should partition two-digit numbers in ways other than place value "to prepare them to solve addition and subtraction calculations involving two-digit numbers", for example 45 + 23 as 45 + 20 + 3.

### Fluency the guidance expects by the end of Year 2
- Addition and subtraction facts within 10, with automatic recall and no counting on, "through continued practice" (2NF–1). These facts are needed for across-10 and larger calculations.
- Doubles of 6, 7, 8 and 9, and halves of 12, 14, 16 and 18 (2AS–1).
- Adding and subtracting across 10 "with sufficient fluency to make progress with the year 3 curriculum" (2AS–1, calculation and fluency).
- The 2, 5 and 10 multiplication tables, by skip counting up to 10 groups "until they are fluent", and then "isolated multiplication facts (for example, 4 fives are 20)" (2MD–1, 2MD–2).
- Using known facts within 10 to solve two-digit calculations "efficiently" (2AS–3).
- The DfE does not give one consolidated end-of-year fluency list. 2AS–3 refers to an appendix for the full set of addition and subtraction facts; that appendix is not in the extracted text.

### Not required in Year 2 (guidance wording)
- Formal written methods for addition and subtraction: "Pupils do not need to learn formal written methods for addition and subtraction in year 2".
- Naming angle types or naming parallel and perpendicular sides: "Pupils don't need to be able to identify or name angle types or parallel/perpendicular sides in year 2".
- Names of regular polyhedra such as octahedrons and dodecahedrons: "year 2 pupils do not need to remember the names of these shapes".
- Using manipulatives, drawn arrays or number lines to calculate: pupils "should not need to use them as methods for carrying out calculations".

## 5. End-of-Year-2 checklist for a parent

These are plain-English "can do" lines based on the statements above. The DfE says a right answer alone does not show a criterion is met (2NF–1, 2AS–3), so use these lines to notice what needs practice, not as a test.

1. I can count forwards and backwards in steps of 2, 3 and 5 from 0, and in 10s from any number. [Y2-NPV-1]
2. I can say what each digit in a two-digit number is worth, for example "42 is 4 tens and 2 ones". [Y2-NPV-2, 2NPV–1]
3. I can say which two multiples of 10 a number is between, for example 79 is between 70 and 80, and one less than 80. [Y2-NPV-3, 2NPV–2]
4. I can compare and order numbers up to 100 with <, > and =, and read and write them in digits and in words. [Y2-NPV-4, Y2-NPV-5]
5. I know my addition and subtraction facts within 10, for example 7 + 3 and 10 − 7, without counting on my fingers. [2NF–1, Y2-AS-2]
6. I can add and subtract across 10, for example 8 + 5 and 13 − 5. [2AS–1, Y2-AS-2]
7. I can add or take away ones, or tens, from a two-digit number, for example 64 + 3 or 75 − 30. [2AS–3, Y2-AS-3]
8. I can add two two-digit numbers, for example 45 + 23, by adding the tens and then the ones. [2AS–4, Y2-AS-3]
9. I can answer "how many more" questions, for example "I have £19 and need £25. How much more do I need?" [2AS–2, Y2-AS-1]
10. I know that 2 + 5 gives the same answer as 5 + 2, and I can check a subtraction by adding. [Y2-AS-4, Y2-AS-5]
11. I can say the 2, 5 and 10 times tables, and tell whether a number is odd or even. [Y2-MD-1, 2MD–1]
12. I can turn equal groups into a multiplication, for example 3 groups of 5 is 3 × 5 = 15, and work out how many bags of 5 make 15. [Y2-MD-2, Y2-MD-4, 2MD–1, 2MD–2]
13. I can find a half, a third, a quarter and three-quarters of a shape, a set or a number, for example a half of 6 is 3, and I know 2/4 is the same as 1/2. [Y2-F-1, Y2-F-2]
14. I can choose a sensible unit and tool, such as a ruler, scales, a thermometer or a measuring jug, and compare lengths, masses or capacities using >, < and =. [Y2-M-1, Y2-M-2]
15. I can name coins, make an amount with them, find different coins that make the same amount, and work out change from a simple purchase. [Y2-M-3, Y2-M-4, Y2-M-5]
16. I can tell and draw the time to five minutes, including quarter past and quarter to, and I know there are 60 minutes in an hour and 24 hours in a day. [Y2-M-6, Y2-M-7, Y2-M-8]
17. I can describe a 2-D shape by counting its sides and vertices, and I can spot a vertical line of symmetry. [Y2-G-1, 2G–1]
18. I can count the edges, vertices and faces of a cuboid or a triangle-based pyramid, and find 2-D shapes on the faces of 3-D shapes. [Y2-G-2, Y2-G-3]
19. I can sort shapes and everyday objects, and describe a quarter, half or three-quarter turn, clockwise and anti-clockwise. [Y2-G-4, Y2-PD-1, Y2-PD-2]
20. I can read and make a pictogram, tally chart or simple table, and answer "how many" and "how many more" questions about it. [Y2-S-1, Y2-S-2, Y2-S-3]
